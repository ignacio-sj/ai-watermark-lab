"I'm stuck," said Maria, dropping her bag on the desk. "My professor wants primary sources for this paper, but I don't even know where to start looking."

"That's a great question to bring to a librarian," said Mr. Okafor, smiling. "First, tell me what a primary source means to you."

"Something original, I guess? Not someone else's analysis of it."

"Exactly. Letters, diaries, government records, newspaper articles from the time, photographs, speeches — anything created during the period you're studying, by someone who was actually there. What's your topic?"

"The 1960s civil rights movement, specifically the Selma marches."

"Perfect. Let's start with our digital archives. We subscribe to databases like JSTOR and Chronicling America, which has digitized newspapers from that era. You could search Selma marches directly in the newspaper archive and read how it was covered in real time."

"I didn't know newspapers counted as primary sources."

"They do, as long as they're reporting events contemporaneously rather than analyzing them decades later. Also check our special collections catalog — we have an oral history archive with interviews from movement participants."

"That sounds perfect. What about actual documents, like FBI records or letters?"

"For those, try the National Archives website, and there's also the Library of Congress's civil rights collection online. Both let you search and view scanned originals for free."

"Should I cite the database or the original document?"

"Cite the original document — author, date, title — then add the database as your access point. I can show you the citation format if you'd like."

"Yes, please. And thank you, this is way less overwhelming now."

"That's what we're here for. Come back if you hit another wall."
