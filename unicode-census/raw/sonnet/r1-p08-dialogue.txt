"Excuse me, I'm working on a paper about immigration in the 1900s, and my professor wants primary sources. I don't really know where to start."

"That's a great topic. Have you used our digitized newspaper archive before?"

"No, I didn't even know we had one."

"We do—it covers local and national papers going back to the 1850s. You can search by keyword and date range, so you could look up terms like 'immigrant,' 'Ellis Island,' or the name of a specific neighborhood."

"That sounds perfect. What else counts as a primary source?"

"Letters, diaries, government records, photographs, census data, court documents—anything created during the time period you're studying, by someone who experienced it directly. Since you're looking at immigration, you might also check the National Archives' digitized ship manifests and naturalization records."

"Is that something I can access from here?"

"Yes, through our library's database portal. I'll show you—there's a link under 'History Resources' on the library website, or you can use one of the terminals here."

"Should I also look for oral histories?"

"Definitely, if you can find them. Many university archives have recorded interviews with immigrants from that era. Our interlibrary loan system can request materials from other institutions if we don't have something locally."

"This is really helpful. One more question—how do I know if something actually counts as a primary source and not just an old article about the topic?"

"Good instinct to check. Ask yourself: did the creator witness or participate in the event, or are they analyzing it afterward? A newspaper article reporting on an event as it happened is primary; a modern historian's book about that event is secondary."

"That makes sense. Thank you so much."

"Anytime. Come back if you get stuck navigating the databases."
