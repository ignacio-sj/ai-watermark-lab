"Excuse me — is this the reference desk?"

"It is. What are you working on?"

"A paper on the 1918 flu in this city. My professor said half my sources have to be primary, and I'm not totally sure what counts."

"Fair question. A primary source is anything produced at the time, by someone with direct contact with the event. A newspaper from October 1918. A health board minute book. A letter home. A photograph. A death register."

"So not a history book about the flu."

"Right — that's secondary. Though secondary books are still useful to you. Go to the back of one and read the footnotes. The author already found the archives; the notes tell you where they are."

"That feels like cheating."

"It's called mining the bibliography, and every historian does it. Now — have you looked at the newspaper databases?"

"I searched Google."

"Try the library's databases instead. We have three that cover 1918 papers, all full-text searchable. Search 'influenza' with a date range narrowed to that autumn, and add the local paper by name. Broad searches will drown you."

"What about things that aren't newspapers?"

"That's where the finding aid comes in. Special Collections upstairs holds the city health department papers. There's a finding aid online — it's an inventory, box by box, folder by folder. You read that first and decide what you want pulled, before you ever go up."

"Do I need an appointment?"

"Yes, and bring ID. Pencils only, no bags. They'll photograph things for you if handling is restricted."

"Is there a shortcut? I have two weeks."

"Two weeks is plenty if you narrow first. Don't write about the epidemic. Write about one school closure, or one hospital, or one week. Narrow questions have findable sources."

"That's actually a relief."

"Start with the newspapers this afternoon. Come back Thursday and tell me what you found — then we'll pick the archive boxes together."

"Thank you. Really."

"That's the job."
